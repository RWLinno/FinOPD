\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Financial time-series forecasting.}
Deep learning approaches to financial time-series prediction have evolved from recurrent networks~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} through attention-based architectures~\cite{vaswani2017attention} to recent models tailored for temporal structure. PatchTST~\cite{nie2023patchtst} segments time series into patches for efficient Transformer processing, while TimesNet~\cite{wu2023timesnet} captures multi-periodicity via 2D convolutions on reshaped temporal data. iTransformer~\cite{liu2024itransformer} inverts the standard attention mechanism to model variate correlations directly. Decoder-only foundation models~\cite{das2024decoder} extend the paradigm to zero-shot forecasting across domains. While these methods achieve strong results on numerical benchmarks, they process raw OHLCV vectors without leveraging the spatial-relational structure that emerges when financial data is rendered as a chart.

\paragraph{Vision-language models and chart understanding.}
General-purpose vision-language models such as GPT-4V~\cite{openai2023gpt4v}, LLaVA~\cite{liu2024llava}, and Qwen-VL~\cite{bai2023qwenvl} have demonstrated strong chart comprehension on benchmarks like ChartQA~\cite{masry2022chartqa} and ChartMimic~\cite{chen2024chartmimic}. These evaluations, however, target generic chart types (bar charts, line plots) and focus on question answering rather than decision-making. In the financial domain, chart images have been used as inputs to convolutional networks for return prediction~\cite{jiang2023cnncharts,chen2021deeplearningcharts}, but these approaches treat the chart as an opaque pixel array without extracting interpretable geometric features such as trendlines, support and resistance levels, or candlestick formations. Our work bridges this gap by combining VLM-based holistic chart interpretation with rule-based geometric extraction, producing structured representations that downstream agents can reason over.

\paragraph{LLM-based financial agents.}
The intersection of large language models and financial decision-making has produced several multi-agent frameworks. FinCon~\cite{fincon2024} organizes LLM agents in a manager-analyst hierarchy coordinated through conceptual verbal reinforcement, achieving strong performance on stock trading tasks using textual and numerical data. TradingAgents~\cite{tradingagents2024} structures agents as fundamental analysts, sentiment analysts, and technical analysts with explicit Bull-Bear debate, demonstrating that adversarial reasoning can improve trading outcomes. FinGPT~\cite{yang2024fingpt} and BloombergGPT~\cite{wu2023bloomberggpt} provide domain-specific language models for financial text understanding, while FinRL~\cite{liu2021finrl} applies deep reinforcement learning to automated trading. Lo et al.~\cite{lo2000foundations} established computational foundations for technical analysis, providing theoretical grounding for pattern-based trading. These systems primarily process textual and numerical data; none treats structured visual chart reasoning as a first-class component of the decision pipeline.

\paragraph{Multi-agent systems for time series.}
MAS4TS~\cite{mas4ts2025} introduces a multi-agent framework for general time-series tasks, employing an Analyzer-Reasoner-Executor paradigm with VLM-based visual reasoning over time-series plots and gated cross-attention communication through shared memory. While MAS4TS demonstrates the value of visual anchors and multi-agent coordination for forecasting and anomaly detection, it targets general time-series analysis rather than financial decision-making. It does not address domain-specific chart geometry (candlestick formations, support and resistance zones, volume patterns), the cognitive decomposition of trader reasoning, or evaluation under realistic financial constraints including transaction costs, slippage, and execution delay.

\paragraph{Technical analysis and pattern recognition.}
Systematic technical analysis has a long history in financial practice, with Murphy~\cite{murphy1999technical} and Bulkowski~\cite{bulkowski2005encyclopedia} providing comprehensive taxonomies of chart patterns and their historical success rates. Nison~\cite{nison2001japanese} formalized candlestick charting techniques that originated in Japanese rice trading. Computational approaches to automated pattern detection have employed template matching, convolutional networks~\cite{jiang2023cnncharts}, and order-book microstructure models~\cite{cohen2024deeplob}. Integration of these classical techniques with modern multi-agent reasoning systems under rigorous financial evaluation, however, remains largely unexplored.

\paragraph{Positioning of this work.}
\textsc{FinVL-MAS} differs from prior work along three axes. First, it treats chart geometry as structured semantic input rather than raw pixels or numerical vectors, combining rule-based precision with VLM-based holistic interpretation. Second, its five-agent decomposition is cognitively motivated, mapping to identifiable stages of expert trader reasoning (perception, pattern recognition, contextual integration, risk assessment, decision formation) rather than adopting generic agent roles. Third, it evaluates under realistic financial constraints with systematic ablations that isolate the contribution of visual reasoning, multi-agent coordination, and gated communication across different market regimes.
